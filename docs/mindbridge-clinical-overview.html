Mindbridge Clinical & research overview v1.0 · August 2026

What happens between sessions

A clinician sees a patient for fifty minutes a month and asks them to remember the other forty-three thousand. Mindbridge is a prototype that closes some of that gap — collecting day-to-day wellbeing data between appointments and turning it into a short, evidence-linked briefing the clinician reads before the session starts.

Audience Clinicians, researchers, service leads Status Working prototype Reading time ~12 minutes
READ FIRST

Mindbridge is a research prototype. It is not a medical device, it has not been clinically validated, and it must not be used to make care decisions for real patients in its current form. It does not diagnose, and it is designed so that it cannot: see §4. Sections 7–9 set out what would have to be true before any of this touched real care.

SECTION 1

The clinical problem: retrospective recall is a poor instrument

Outpatient psychiatry and psychotherapy run on episodic contact. A patient seen monthly is asked, at each appointment, to summarise four weeks of internal experience from memory. That summary is the primary evidence on which the next month of care is planned.

This is a known measurement problem, not a failure of individual patients. Retrospective self-report is shaped by recall bias — recent days and emotionally extreme days are over-weighted, ordinary days compress, and current mood colours the recollection of past mood. A patient who is well on the day of the appointment tends to under-report a bad fortnight; a patient who is struggling that morning may report the whole month as uniformly bad. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) — asking people to report in the moment, repeatedly, in daily life — was developed in response to exactly this, and is a well-established research method.

Three things are typically lost between appointments:

  • Temporal structure. Not "sleep was bad" but which nights, in what order, and what happened around them.
  • Collateral observation. What a partner or family member noticed, which routinely diverges from self-report in clinically meaningful ways.
  • Non-events. The days nothing was recorded at all — which may be the most informative days of the month.

Mindbridge is an attempt to make that period legible without asking the clinician to read thirty days of raw charts before every appointment.

SECTION 2

What Mindbridge does

The system has one loop. A patient logs short daily check-ins between appointments. Before the next session, the clinician generates a briefing. The briefing summarises the window, names specific dates, links what was measured to what was written, and proposes things to ask about — each claim tied to a date or a number in the underlying record.

The clinician's view and the patient's view are deliberately different:

Access is asymmetric by design. The rationale is in §11 — it is a real trade-off, not an oversight.
ElementClinicianPatient
Own check-ins, journal, chartsYesYes
Check-in coverage / gapsYesYes
Clinician's baseline severity ratingYesNo
Partner / observer streamYesNo
AI briefingYesNo

The reasoning: a patient who can see the clinician's severity rating, or their partner's parallel account, is answering a different question when they log their mood. Showing it would change what is reported, and the report is the measurement. That reasoning is contestable and is revisited in §11.

SECTION 3

The four data streams

A patient record is assembled from four streams. Each carries a provenance tag, so any row can be traced to where it came from.

Daily check-inSelf-reported

One mood rating (1–10) plus free tags, once per day. Re-saving replaces the day rather than stacking entries.

JournalSelf-reported

Free text, written by the patient in their own words. This is what gives the numbers meaning.

PhysiologicalSimulated

Sleep duration and quality, resting heart rate, HRV, respiration rate. Currently synthetic — see §6.

ObserverSimulated

A partner or family member's parallel rating and notes about the same days. Divergence is the signal.

Measured fields

Ranges are enforced at the import boundary; out-of-range values are rejected per-row with the exact path to the offending field.

Coverage is a measurement, not a grade

The system reports check-in coverage: of the days in the tracked window, how many carry a check-in, and precisely which dates are missing. This is surfaced to the clinician as a data-quality caveat — a quiet fortnight with 40% coverage is not evidence of a quiet fortnight.

Design note

The briefing is instructed to frame gaps as a limitation of the data, never as non-compliance by the patient. The reason a check-in was missed is often clinically interesting in itself, and framing it as a failure makes it less likely to be discussed.

SECTION 4

What the AI may not do

The language model is given a narrow job: describe patterns in data. It is instructed, in absolute terms, that it must never name, suggest, imply or rule out any diagnosis; never recommend or adjust medication, dose or treatment; and never interpret a pattern as illness. Suggestions must be phrased as things to explore — "consider asking about…", "worth monitoring…" — and every claim must trace to a date or a number in the supplied record.

Three mechanisms enforce this, in order of importance:

  1. The instruction itself. The primary control. The model is scoped to pattern description and given an explicit output schema.
  2. A post-hoc keyword screen. Every generated briefing is scanned for diagnostic and prescribing language — diagnostic terms, named disorders, dosing verbs. A flagged briefing is still saved and shown, with a banner naming the matched wording. It is a cheap smoke alarm, not a safety system, and is documented as such.
  3. Visual separation in the interface. Model-generated interpretation is rendered in marked blocks labelled AI interpretation, visually distinct from unmarked panels showing recorded data. The clinician can always see which is which.
Limitation, stated plainly

A keyword screen catches vocabulary, not meaning. A model can describe a diagnosis perfectly while avoiding every listed word. The screen reduces obvious failures; it does not make the output safe. The clinician reading the briefing is the control that matters, and the system is built on the assumption that a qualified human reads every word before it informs anything.

SECTION 5

A real briefing, unedited

Daniel Okafor, 34 2 Jul – 31 Jul 2026 · 30 days · coverage 57% (13 days missing)
AI interpretation — summary

Over the tracked window of 2026-07-02 to 2026-07-31, sleep remained generally low (average ≈4.2 hrs) with extreme lows of 0.4 hrs on 2026-07-18 and 2.2 hrs on 2026-07-23. Self-rated mood fluctuated between 1 and 7, frequently falling to 1–3, especially mid-month and late month. Anxiety repeatedly reached 8–10… Partner observations often rated the patient lower than self-report and noted withdrawal or flat affect on days such as 2026-07-06, 2026-07-11, 2026-07-17 and 2026-07-28.

AI interpretation — findings

Severe sleep disruption coincides with low mood and partner-observed withdrawal

Sleep dropped to 0.4 hrs on July 18 and 2.2 hrs on July 23, with self-rated mood of 1 on both days and partner notes of being withdrawn or "flat".

sleep_hours: 0.4 on 2026-07-18, 2.2 on 2026-07-23 · mood_1_10: 1 on both dates · partner journal 2026-07-17, 2026-07-28 describing withdrawal

Partner observations frequently rate the patient lower than self-report

On days such as July 13 (self mood 5 vs partner 4) and July 28 (self 4 vs partner 3) the partner rated the patient lower, often coinciding with self-reported hopeless or numb tags.

patient mood_1_10: 5 on 2026-07-13, 4 on 2026-07-28 · partner mood: 4 and 3 · patient tags [ok] on 2026-07-28 vs partner tags [anxious, low]

AI interpretation — suggested discussion points

"Consider exploring discrepancies between self-report and partner observations on days such as July 13 and July 28 to understand gaps in awareness." — rationale: partner frequently rated the patient lower than self-report on days tagged hopeless or numb.

✓ Diagnostic-language screen: PASSED — 0 matches

What is worth noticing is the fourth finding. Nobody wrote a rule that says "compare the two accounts and flag divergence" — the discrepancy detection is an emergent read of two parallel streams. It is also exactly the kind of observation that is easy to miss when scrolling thirty days of charts, and exactly the kind that changes what a clinician asks in the room.

Equally worth noticing: every claim carries its own receipts. A clinician who distrusts a finding can check it against the recorded data in seconds, which is the only reasonable posture toward a language model in a clinical setting.

SECTION 6

Knowing what is real

The prototype needed patient histories before any patient existed, so it includes a data generator: a statistical model that produces plausible 30-day histories across three documented severity bands, with journal text drawn from a pool of 48 hand-written entries.

This is the single most dangerous thing in the system, because fabricated clinical data that looks real is fabricated clinical data. It is handled by making provenance non-optional — every row carries a source tag, and the generator is never triggered automatically:

A new patient starts empty. Generated data is only ever produced by an explicit, deliberate action.
TagOriginTrustworthy?
self-reported The patient, through the portal Real
imported Validated JSON from another system Real
generated The simulator, on explicit request Synthetic

The generator remains useful for three legitimate purposes: demonstrating the loop without exposing patient data, load-testing, and — most importantly — adversarial testing of the briefing. Because a synthetic history has known ground truth, it can be used to ask whether the model finds the pattern that was deliberately planted, and whether it invents patterns that were not. That is a genuine evaluation instrument, and it is the reason the generator is worth keeping.

Tell-tale

Generated journal text repeats across a 30-day window, because the pool holds 48 entries. If you are reading a record and the same sentence appears three times, you are reading synthetic data. Real entries never repeat verbatim.

SECTION 7

Taking it into real care

The prototype already runs on real infrastructure: a live relational database and a live language model, with the patient portal producing genuine self-reported entries. Three things stand between that and a system usable in a service.

7.1  Replace simulated physiology with real device data

The physiological stream is the main synthetic component. The realistic route is consumer wearables the patient already owns, read through their vendor APIs — sleep staging, resting heart rate, HRV and respiration are all standard exports. A single normalising layer would map each vendor's shape onto the internal schema, which is why that schema is deliberately vendor-neutral.

Three caveats a researcher will raise immediately, and should:

  • Consumer wearables are not clinical instruments. Sleep staging in particular is derived and vendor-specific; two devices will disagree about the same night. Treat these as trend signals within a person, never as absolute values or between-person comparisons.
  • Device ownership is a confounder. Restricting a study to people who own a wearable selects for income, age and digital literacy, and will bias any finding.
  • Missingness is not random. A device goes uncharged during exactly the weeks a patient is least well. The gap and the deterioration are correlated, which is a serious analytic hazard and also, clinically, a signal in itself.

7.2  Replace ad-hoc scales with validated instruments

The 1–10 mood, anxiety and energy ratings are convenient and unvalidated. They carry no psychometric properties, no established cut-offs, no norms, and cannot be compared across studies or services. For any real use they should sit alongside — or be replaced by — instruments already validated for the population in question, administered at their intended frequency rather than daily.

A defensible design keeps a very short daily item for temporal resolution and adds a periodic validated instrument for measurement, rather than pretending a daily 1–10 slider is a scale. Selecting those instruments is a clinical and psychometric decision, not an engineering one, and should be made with the service and its population.

7.3  Build the escalation path that does not currently exist

This is the most important gap. The prototype has no risk detection and no escalation route. A patient can write something indicating acute risk into the journal at two in the morning, and the system will file it quietly and show it to the clinician at the next appointment, which may be four weeks away.

Blocking requirement

A between-session monitoring tool creates an implied promise that someone is watching. If nobody is, the tool is worse than nothing — a patient may disclose to the app instead of to a person. Before any real deployment this needs: crisis resources shown at the point of writing, a defined monitoring window with an explicit statement of what is not monitored, a triage route to a human, and an audited escalation protocol agreed with the service. None of this is technically hard. All of it is mandatory.

SECTION 8

Safety, ethics and regulation

Data protection

Mental-health records are special category data under UK/EU GDPR and protected health information under HIPAA. The current prototype does not meet the bar and does not pretend to: it uses in-memory sessions, has no rate limiting or account lockout, no field-level encryption, and no audit trail. A real deployment needs encryption in transit and at rest, role-based access control, a complete audit log of every record access, defined retention and erasure, a Data Protection Impact Assessment, and — where a language model is involved — a clear, documented answer to where the text goes and whether it can be retained or trained on.

Current deployment

The prototype sends patient text to a third-party model provider over the public internet, and its database is currently reachable from any host. Both are acceptable for a prototype holding synthetic data. Both are disqualifying for real patient data. Self-hosting the model, or contracting for a zero-retention endpoint, is a precondition rather than an optimisation.

Consent

Consent must be specific and revocable, and must cover the parts patients do not expect: that an automated system reads their journal; that a partner may contribute observations about them; that a clinician sees a rating they cannot see themselves. The observer stream needs its own consent from both parties, and a clear position on what happens to it when a relationship ends. Withdrawal must be genuinely available without affecting care.

Regulatory position

Software that informs clinical decisions is likely to fall within the medical device definition in most jurisdictions — Software as a Medical Device under UKCA/CE marking, or the FDA's SaMD framework. The determining question is what the software claims. "Displays data the patient recorded" sits differently from "identifies patients at risk of deterioration". The current framing — decision support, no diagnosis, clinician-in-the-loop — is the more conservative position, but classification is a regulatory judgement to be taken formally and early, not asserted in a document like this one. In an NHS context, DTAC would apply alongside.

SECTION 9

A validation pathway

These phases are genuinely sequential: each answers a question that makes the next one worth running, and a failure at any stage should stop the work rather than be designed around.

  1. Technical and analytic validation

    No patients. Using synthetic records with known ground truth, measure whether the briefing finds planted patterns, how often it fabricates patterns that are not there, and how stable its output is across repeated runs on identical input.

    Gate: an acceptable and quantified fabrication rate. Nothing proceeds without this number.

  2. Clinical face validity

    Clinicians blind-rate briefings against the underlying records: is it accurate, is it useful, would it change what you ask? Include briefings generated from records with no real signal, to test whether the system manufactures narrative from noise.

    Gate: clinicians can distinguish informative briefings from empty ones.

  3. Feasibility and acceptability

    A small cohort of consenting patients, with full safety scaffolding in place. Measures: sustained adherence over weeks, attrition, patient-reported burden, effect on the therapeutic relationship, and clinician time cost per patient.

    Gate: adherence that does not collapse after the novelty period, and no harm to the alliance.

  4. Clinical utility

    Only now a controlled question: does access to the briefing change clinical decisions, and do those changes improve outcomes on validated measures against standard care?

    Gate: the honest possibility of a null result, reported.

Phase 1 is the phase most often skipped and the one that matters most here, because a language model that confabulates a date or a correlation produces output that is fluent, specific, evidence-shaped and wrong — the hardest kind of error for a busy reader to catch.

SECTION 10

What it could be worth

These are hypotheses the design is built to test, not demonstrated outcomes. Nothing below has been shown for this system.

Session time spent better

If the first ten minutes no longer go on reconstructing the month, they go somewhere else. The briefing arrives with dates already identified, so the conversation can start at "tell me about the eighteenth".

Divergence made visible

Systematic gaps between self-report and observer report are clinically meaningful and easy to miss. Surfacing them with specific dates turns an impression into something discussable.

Caseload triage

Across a caseload, coverage and trend direction offer a defensible way to decide who to contact sooner — a scheduling aid, not a clinical judgement.

Patients see their own pattern

The portal shows patients their own trends. For some, seeing that a bad week was a bad week and not a bad life is itself therapeutic, and it supports self-monitoring skills taught in most structured therapies.

Research-grade structure

Timestamped, provenance-tagged, exportable longitudinal data with explicit missingness is a usable substrate for research — which is rarer in routine services than it should be.

Data quality made honest

Because coverage is reported alongside every briefing, the clinician always knows how much of the window they are actually seeing. Most systems quietly present partial data as complete.

SECTION 11

Risks and open questions

Automation bias

A fluent, confident briefing is persuasive regardless of accuracy. The risk is that it anchors clinical thinking before the clinician forms their own view. Visual separation of interpretation from data helps; it does not solve it. Presenting the briefing after the clinician's own review, rather than before, is worth testing.

Surveillance and the therapeutic alliance

Daily monitoring plus an observer stream can feel like being watched. That perception may suppress honest reporting — degrading the measurement — or alter the relationship in ways that matter more than any data gained. This is an empirical question for phase 3, and the design should be prepared to lose.

The asymmetry problem

Patients cannot see their severity rating, their partner's entries, or the briefing written about them. The rationale (§2) is measurement integrity. The counter-argument is that this is their health record, that professional opacity has a poor history in mental health care, and that in some jurisdictions they have a legal right of access regardless. This remains genuinely unresolved and should be decided with patient representatives, not by engineers.

Quantification changes what is attended to

What gets measured gets discussed. A system that measures sleep, heart rate and mood ratings may quietly shift sessions toward what is measurable and away from meaning, relationship and circumstance — which is where most of the clinical work actually happens.

Model drift and reproducibility

The provider's model can change beneath the system without notice, altering behaviour with no code change. Briefings therefore record which model produced them and snapshot the data they analysed, so a saved briefing stays interpretable. Any serious deployment needs a pinned, versioned model and re-validation on change.

Technical appendix

Two implementation details with clinical consequences

Briefings snapshot their evidence. A saved briefing stores the data it analysed, not a reference to it. Regenerating or re-importing a patient's data does not retrospectively alter a briefing that has already been read and acted upon. This costs storage and is the correct trade.

There is a deterministic fallback. If every model provider fails, the system produces a briefing from a local statistical pass — trends across the window, Pearson correlations between streams, highest-burden days, observer divergence, coverage gaps. It is derived from the patient's actual data, labels itself as non-model output, and means a provider outage never leaves a clinician with an empty screen mid-session.

Interoperability

The import format is bespoke JSON. For any real deployment, alignment with FHIR resources — Observation for measurements, QuestionnaireResponse for instruments — would be the appropriate direction, and would make integration with an existing electronic record a configuration exercise rather than a project.
